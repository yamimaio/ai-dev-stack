# Test a local builder against the review bill

*Report date 2026-11-11. Built from five research notes gathered 2026-11-10, plus a check of the Gravity clone (`main` at `d43652c`, 2026-11-10). No model was downloaded or run for this report.*

A local builder pilot on your M1 Max is feasible now, and it is worth running as a measured pilot on a narrow set of issues, not as a rollout. The recommended setup is **Qwen3.6-35B-A3B or its Claude Code-tuned fine-tune KAT-Coder-V2.5 (Stage 1 picks one; Yami, 2026-11-12) at 4-bit (GGUF) served by llama.cpp's `llama-server` on the macOS host**. It is driven by **a pinned, separately launched Claude Code pointed at that local endpoint**, so CLAUDE.md, `/build-issue` and the `.claude/settings.json` deny rules carry over unchanged. Claude keeps planning and reviewing on your subscription in separate sessions, and no router is involved. Expect a model that is about a Claude generation behind. In a Claude Code harness, the best Mac-sized model scored **64.4% on SWE-bench Verified** [third party]. No Mac-sized model has been run on Terminal-Bench 4.1, where **Opus 5.5 scores 65.8%** and the best open-weight model of any size scores 41.2%. Expect **1-3 minutes of prompt processing on cold turns**, and the most common failures are tool-call and chat-template problems and "false done" reports. The deciding fact is economic, not technical. Your Opus-built PRs already need a **median of 2 review rounds**, and only about **6 of 61 pass round 1** (my notes' regex count). Every extra round a weaker builder causes is paid from the same Claude usage the swap is meant to save. Nobody has measured the build-versus-review share of that usage for Gravity. Measure it first, and soon: Claude Code deletes local session logs after 30 days, so the window for an Opus baseline closes day by day.

*Evidence labels used throughout: **[measured]** controlled test or telemetry; **[vendor]** the model maker's own number; **[third party]** someone other than the maker ran it; **[anecdote]** one person's run with numbers; **[opinion]** a source's judgment; **[inference]** my reasoning from the cited facts, not measured.*

## Swapping only the builder is the textbook first move, but the review bill decides it

Splitting planner, builder and reviewer across models is an established pattern, and the builder ("editor") role is the one that tolerates a weaker model best. Aider's 2024 architect/editor benchmark showed an open-weight DeepSeek editor improving most architects. For example, o1-preview as architect with DeepSeek as editor scored **85.0% against o1-preview solo at 79.7%** [measured, Sept 2024, outdated] ([Aider](https://aider.chat/2024/09/26/architect.html)). Those were single-file edit exercises with hosted models. Gravity's builder runs a multi-step tool loop over a large repo under strict written rules, so the Aider gains say little about your case [inference]. By September 2026 practitioners describe two routing shapes. In **Pattern 1** a frontier model plans and reviews while cheaper workers edit. In **Pattern 2** a cheap model drives the loop and a frontier "advisor" checks in at set checkpoints: after the plan, after repeated test failures, and before the PR opens [opinion] ([Arize, 2026-09-08](https://arize.com/blog/how-i-cut-coding-agent-costs-with-model-harness-routing/)). Your current setup, with Claude planning, a session building and a fresh session reviewing, is already Pattern 1 with a human gate. Swapping only the builder is the standard first step, and Pattern 2 is the natural second, because `/build-issue` already has those checkpoints [inference].

Local agentic coding crossed a threshold this year, but only for small tasks. On one developer's simple refactor eval (extract a helper, tests must pass), **no local model succeeded in December 2025 (0/10)**. By **April 2026, Qwen 3.5 35B-A3B and Gemma 4 26B-A4B each scored about 9/10**, against 80-100% for frontier models [measured, small eval, M4 Pro 48 GB] ([Couch, Dec 2025](https://www.simonpcouch.com/blog/2025-12-04-local-agents); [Couch, Apr 2026](https://simonpcouch.com/blog/2026-04-16-local-agents-2/)). The same author says Gemma 4 still "does stupid shit all the time", needs steering "like Sonnet 4-era Claude Code", and is not "a broadly competent partner like Opus 4.6" [opinion]. The closest report to your hardware is a January 2026 run of Claude Code with GLM-4.7-Flash via Ollama on an **M1 Max 64 GB**. Each tool call took about 2 minutes against 2-3 seconds in the cloud, so an investigation took **1h 22m locally against 1m 13s**, about 68x slower, with "nearly indistinguishable" output [anecdote] ([paddo.dev](https://paddo.dev/blog/claude-code-local-ollama/)). That run predates the prompt-cache fix discussed below, and 2 minutes per call fits a model re-reading the whole prompt on every turn. The 68x is therefore probably a worst case, not a floor [inference]. Across sources the failure modes repeat. Models call no tools, or print the call as text. They pass wrong tool arguments, retry the same failing call in a loop, or burn the output budget on reasoning tokens. Worst for you, a model can **declare success while the work is incomplete**: Qwen 3 14B wrote the helper, never wired it in, and reported done [measured] ([Couch, Dec 2025](https://www.simonpcouch.com/blog/2025-12-04-local-agents); [kuroko1t](https://dev.to/kuroko1t/what-happens-when-local-llms-fail-at-tool-calling-testing-7-models-with-a-rust-coding-agent-cep)). Gravity adds one more failure mode that no source measured: forgetting a 17 KB rulebook, or ploughing through a design decision instead of flagging it and leaving it undone [inference].

For someone aiming at a Head, Director or VP of Engineering role with an AI-adoption focus, the pilot is worth as much as a case study in measurement as it is in savings. Four pieces of evidence set the frame. First, **perception does not match measurement**. METR's 2025 trial found experienced developers **19% slower** with AI (95% CI +2% to +39%) while they believed they were faster. Its February 2026 follow-up calls its own newer data "an unreliable signal", because 30-50% of developers withheld tasks they did not want to do without AI [measured] ([METR, 2026-02-24](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Second, **review is the hidden cost**. Faros telemetry across 22,000 developers found that high-AI-adoption teams have **54% more bugs per PR and 441.5% longer median review time** [measured, May 2026] ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-code-quality-senior-engineer-review-burden)). DORA 2025 found AI raises throughput but also instability, and calls AI an amplifier of strong or weak organisations [measured, survey] ([InfoQ on DORA 2025](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/09/dora-state-of-ai-in-dev-2025)). Third, **the harness is a lever as large as the model**. One model across four harnesses passed between 14 and 17 of 30 tasks, and cost per success varied about 2.7x [measured, cited second-hand, "directional"] ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/how-i-cut-coding-agent-costs-with-model-harness-routing/)). Fourth, the accepted measurement grammar is **cost per accepted outcome**, not per token [opinion] (Arize). DX's framework stages it as utilization, then impact, then cost, and warns against using these metrics for individual performance [framework] ([DX](https://getdx.com/ai-measurement-framework/)).

The leadership lessons follow directly [opinion/inference]. Pre-register before you look at results. Measure per merged PR. Treat review load and owner minutes as costs. Verify data locality with a network check instead of assuming it. Be honest about lock-in: open weights reduce model-vendor dependence, but your harness, commands and review process stay Anthropic-shaped. Gravity's strict, written review policy is exactly the "strong organisation" DORA says AI amplifies, and it is also what makes this pilot measurable at all.

Every open-weight model that rivals Claude on coding boards is far too large for 64 GB. These include Kimi K3 (2.8T total, 104B active), DeepSeek V4 Pro (1.6T, 49B active), GLM-5.2 (744B, 40B active) and MiniMax M3 (~230B). Even GLM-5.3-Flash needs about 160 GB at 4-bit ([Morph](https://www.morphllm.com/best-open-source-coding-model-2026); [AiCybr](https://aicybr.com/blog/glm-5-3-flash-local-deployment-guide)). The class that fits is mixture-of-experts (MoE) models of about 30-40B total parameters with about 3B active, plus a few dense models of about 27B. Second, the most relevant figure for you is the **Claude Code-harness 64.4**. On the maintainers' bash-only board (newest rows February 2026), that sits below **Sonnet 4.5 at 71.4 and Opus 4.5 at 76.8**. So the realistic expectation is a builder somewhat below late-2025 Claude, in your exact harness [inference] ([BenchLeader](https://www.benchleader.com/benchmarks/swe_bench_verified)). Third, Terminal-Bench versions do not compare. TB 4.1 is so much harder that **Opus 4.8 scores 23.6%** on it, so a small model's TB 2.0 score of 51.5 predicts little [inference from [BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/terminal-bench-2)]. The only comparison that settles anything is your own: the same harness, your own issues, your own reviewer.

The model's fit on the Mac decides the shortlist. **Qwen3.6-35B-A3B** is about 20.4 GB at GGUF Q4_K_M plus a 1.06 GB MTP head, and about 39 GB at Q8. Only 10 of its 40 layers keep a standard KV cache (2 KV heads, head dim 256), so by my arithmetic from the model card, **64k of context costs about 1.3 GB at fp16** [inference]. That matters because Claude Code wants a large served context (see the harness section). Older-architecture 30B-A3B models need several times more KV memory [inference]. Dense **Qwen3.8-27B** has the best small-model SWE-bench Pro score (61.7, vendor). But on an M1 Max it measured **11.8-19 tok/s decode and 107-134 tok/s prompt processing**, against **54-68 tok/s and 636-892 tok/s** for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. That makes a 30k-token cold prompt about 4 minutes instead of about 35 seconds [measured at a 512-token prompt, M1 Max 32 GB, Sept 2026; long-prompt figures inferred] ([bishal.io](https://bishal.io/blog/six-local-models-m1-max-llama-cpp-vs-mlx/)). **Qwen3-Coder-Next** fits only at about 3-bit (UD-Q3_K_XL 36.3 GB), where measurable quality loss begins, and no measured Mac run exists ([Unsloth GGUF](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen3-Coder-Next-GGUF); [llmcheck](https://llmcheck.net/blog/best-local-llm-for-coding-mac/)). **Recursive Flint** is three weeks old, has only vendor scores, and has a known GGUF chat-template bug. One test saw its speed fall from about 110 tok/s on short prompts to about 9 tok/s near 115K tokens ([OrcaRouter](https://www.orcarouter.ai/blog/flint-v1-0-vs-north-mini-code)). **North Mini Code** is verbose: about 75M output tokens on Artificial Analysis's run against a class median of about 25M ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/north-mini-code-cohere-s-small-coding-focused-moe-model)).

Quantization is not the risk. 2026 studies find **no clear loss in agentic SWE success at 4-bit** against BF16 or FP8. Loss appears at about 2-bit, and 1-bit collapses [measured] ([imec aistack, 2026-10-08](https://aistack.imec-int.com/blog/quantization); [Quesma, 2026-08-26](https://quesma.com/blog/qwen38-27b-quantizations-benchmarked/)). A November 2026 workshop paper adds a caution. Final accuracy held at 4-bit, but trajectories shifted: one model's one-shot solves fell from 17 to 0 while its loop-based solves rose from 10 to 25. So judge tool use and transcripts, not only pass rates [measured, older base models] ([arXiv 2611.02368](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02368)). The real risk is **tool-calling plumbing**. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B has many reports of loops and unparsed tool-call text at Q6, Q8 and even BF16, in LM Studio, Ollama and OpenCode on Mac. Most trace to chat templates, parser bugs and reasoning settings, not to the weights [anecdote] ([HF discussion #5](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-GGUF/discussions/5); [HF discussion #20](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B/discussions/20)). The fixes on offer are the vendor's template, `preserve_thinking`, a llama.cpp `--reasoning-budget`, and Qwen's coding sampling settings (temp 0.6, top_p 0.95, top_k 20) ([Qwen3.6 card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B)). One user reported that the flag `--swa-full` largely fixed the looping; another workaround did not help. Pin the runtime and the template before you blame the model [inference].

## Prompt processing, not generation speed, sets the pace on an M1 Max

An agent turn on Apple Silicon has two phases. **Prefill** is the model reading the whole prompt. It is compute-bound, so it scales with GPU cores, and it is where the M1 lags newer chips most. **Decode** is the model generating tokens one at a time. It is bandwidth-bound, and a 3B-active MoE model reads few bytes per token, so it is fast. At about 8.5K tokens, prefill was **about 94% of total response time** on an M1 Max [anecdote] ([r/LocalLLaMA snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rs059a/snapshots/2026-03-13T23%3A00%3A09.503181Z)).

The M1 Max measurements for 30-35B-A3B models at 4-bit are consistent on decode and inconsistent on prefill [measured]. oMLX on a **32-core-GPU M1 Max** ran Qwen3-30B-A3B at **465 tok/s prefill and 46-59 tok/s decode**, with a 17.1 GB peak (March 2026) ([oMLX benchmark](https://omlx.ai/benchmarks/ov249nz9)). A **24-core-GPU M1 Max** prefilled 8.5K tokens in 49.1 s on GGUF (about **173 tok/s**). The same test found Ollama **37% slower than raw llama.cpp** on the same GGUF engine (before Ollama's MLX backend), and found that converting MLX bf16 weights to fp16 sped prefill about 1.7x on M1, which has no native bf16 ([famstack.dev](https://famstack.dev/guides/mlx-vs-gguf-part-2-isolating-variables/)). Your GPU core count (24 or 32) therefore matters, and you should check it. Planning range: Claude Code's fixed prompt is about 23K tokens. CLAUDE.md (17,011 bytes) and `build-issue.md` (15,221 bytes) add roughly 8K more, and the issue and its cited docs come on top. A **first turn of 31K+ tokens should take about 1-3 minutes** [inference]. Measured data is scarce near that size: no one has published 16-64K prefill for a 30B-A3B model on an M1 Max. A dense 9B model's prefill fell only about 11% between 8.5K and 27K tokens on llama.cpp (452 to 403 tok/s, October 2026) ([lilting.ch](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/m1-max-llm-engine-benchmark)).

**Prefix caching turns minutes into seconds, and Claude Code breaks it by default.** The cache only works when each new prompt begins with exactly the same tokens as the last one. Claude Code puts an attribution/billing header at the start of the system prompt, and its value changes on every request. The Unsloth docs say this makes local inference "90% slower" ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/basics/claude-code)). One Mac Studio setup measured **~100 s warm turns before the fix and 7-8 s after** stripping the header and patching the server's prefix cache [measured, Mac Studio 96 GB, dense 32B] ([dev.to](https://dev.to/vinayiitkgp/why-self-hosted-claude-code-was-15-slower-than-it-should-be-3pb4)). On an M1 Max 64 GB, a fourth turn at about 8K tokens took **~49 s to prefill in LM Studio and ~1.7 s in oMLX** [anecdote] ([r/LocalLLaMA snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rs059a/snapshots/2026-03-13T23%3A00%3A09.503181Z)). The fix is `"CLAUDE_CODE_ATTRIBUTION_HEADER": "0"` in the `env` block of Claude Code's settings (older builds ignored the shell variable), then confirm cache hits in the server log on turn 2. An unread tracker item titled "Claude Code ≥ 2.1.36 forcing full prompt re-processing on llama-server" suggests this can regress between releases ([claudeissues #32636](https://claudeissues.com/issue/32636-bug-claude-code-2-1-36-forcing-full-prompt-re-processing-on-llama-server), title only).

Memory is comfortable for the recommended model but not for its 8-bit version. macOS lets the GPU wire about **75% of unified memory, about 48 GB on 64 GB**, by default. `sudo sysctl iogpu.wired_limit_mb` raises the cap until reboot, and Apple does not support it ([devnote](https://github.com/ivanopcode/devnote-override-macos-metal-vram-cap)). Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at 8-bit peaked at **42-44 GB** on an M1 Max 64 GB [measured] ([sleepyquant](https://dev.to/sleepyquant/mlx-vs-llamacpp-on-m1-max-with-35b-q8-the-honest-benchmark-3496)). That leaves too little for the Docker Desktop VM running `gravity-i<N>` (Postgres plus the app container), a reviewer's `gravity-pr<N>` stack and a browser. Keep model plus cache at or below about 32 GB and leave the sysctl at its default [inference]. Set the context window explicitly, because LM Studio defaults to 4,096 tokens. One source says Ollama's old 4,096 default "silently truncates from the beginning", while Ollama's current docs default by VRAM tier (32k at 24-48 GB, 256k at 48 GB or more). A 64 GB Mac whose GPU cap is about 48 GB sits right on that tier boundary ([RunAIHome](https://runaihome.com/blog/claude-code-ollama-local-models-setup-2026/); [Ollama docs](https://docs.ollama.com/context-length)).

## Claude Code on a local endpoint keeps every guardrail; a router gives up the subscription

The harness choice comes down to one property: **only Claude Code itself enforces Gravity's rules without rewriting them**. Ollama (v0.14+), LM Studio (0.4.1+) and llama.cpp all serve Anthropic's `/v1/messages`, so Claude Code needs no proxy ([Ollama blog](https://ollama.com/blog/claude); [LM Studio blog](https://lmstudio.ai/blog/claudecode)). CLAUDE.md, `/build-issue`, hooks and the `settings.json` deny → ask → allow rules that block host `node`/`npm`/`npx` are all enforced by the harness, not the model, so they carry over as they are ([compatibility matrix, 2026-11-05](https://codylindley.github.io/ai-harness-engineering-compatibility-matrix/)). The costs are real, though. Anthropic "doesn't support routing Claude Code to non-Claude models through any gateway" and warns that each release can add features a gateway does not forward ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/llm-gateway)). The fixed prompt is about 23K tokens: about 5.6K of system prompt and about 17.6K of tool schemas [measured] ([dev.to](https://dev.to/vinayiitkgp/why-self-hosted-claude-code-was-15-slower-than-it-should-be-3pb4)).

A third-party page updated 2026-10-12 lists the breakages to configure around ([developertoolkit.ai](https://developertoolkit.ai/en/ecosystem/agent-tools/gateways-local-models/)). If 400 errors mention `context_management`, set `CLAUDE_CODE_DISABLE_EXPERIMENTAL_BETAS=1`. Point **all four** model aliases at the served model, or background requests to the "Haiku" alias fail. Claude Code does not recognise a local backend's context-limit error, and **`CLAUDE_CODE_AUTO_COMPACT_WINDOW` is clamped to at least 100,000 tokens**. Serve at least 100K of context, or keep sessions short, because Claude Code cannot compact to fit anything smaller. **Remote Control is disabled** when the base URL is not Anthropic's, so builder sessions must run in a terminal on the Mac. Data locality also needs checking, not assuming. The M1 Max user above found that setting only the main model variable **still sent background requests to Anthropic**, and fixed it with a separate launcher [anecdote] ([paddo.dev](https://paddo.dev/blog/claude-code-local-ollama/)). Anthropic's docs add a billing subtlety. Setting `ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL` without a gateway credential leaves the claude.ai login active, while setting a credential replaces the subscription login ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/llm-gateway)). For a local-only builder, that argues for a dummy `ANTHROPIC_AUTH_TOKEN` in a dedicated launcher, and a network check during the session [inference].

Other harnesses have smaller prompts and similar local support, but each needs your rules re-taught. **Codex CLI `--oss`** has a baseline prompt of about 8.5K tokens without MCP and ran Qwen 3.6 35B-A3B cleanly in April 2026 tests. It reads `AGENTS.md`, not CLAUDE.md, its permission profiles are in beta, and its memory feature uses OpenAI-hosted models by default ([Vaughan, Pi vs Codex](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/04/19/does-pi-outperform-codex-cli-for-local-models/); [Vaughan, Codex guide](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/04/24/open-weight-models-codex-cli-local-coding-agents-comparison-guide/)). **Pi** has about a 600-token prompt and 4 tools, and halved wall time for a dense 31B model. But it made "negligible difference on MoE models", and **Qwen 3.6 35B-A3B failed under Pi** by writing files to `/tmp`, reproduced twice [measured, small] (same source). **OpenCode** is the closest non-Claude fit. It reads CLAUDE.md and `~/.claude/skills`, and its shell permission rules check each subcommand. But by default it does not ask before reading or editing outside the project, and it is unconfirmed whether it reads project `.claude/commands` or the `settings.json` deny rules ([OpenCode rules](https://opencode.ai/docs/rules/); [OpenCode permissions](https://opencode.ai/docs/permissions/)). **Goose** (Apache-2.0, now under the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, v1.58.0 on 2026-11-09) has headless recipes but advises keeping hint files "under a page", which your rules exceed ([shattered.io](https://shattered.io/goose-ai-coding-assistant-local-ollama-12-steps-2026/)). **Crush** is source-available (FSL-1.1-MIT) and changed its config format in July 2026 ([localaimaster](https://localaimaster.com/blog/crush-ollama-setup)). **Qwen Code** (added 2026-11-12 after Yami asked; checked against its docs on GitHub `main`) is an alternative built for Qwen models: an Apache-2.0 fork of Gemini CLI, now at a "1.0 Preview". It reads QWEN.md and AGENTS.md, and `context.fileName` can point it at CLAUDE.md; `skills.directories` can load `.claude/skills`; `permissions.deny` uses Claude Code's syntax (e.g. `Bash(rm -rf *)`), though whether it catches subshells and wrappers is unknown; commands live in `.qwen/commands/*.md` with `{{args}}` instead of `$ARGUMENTS`; it speaks OpenAI and Anthropic APIs. Its only published benchmark is SWE-bench Verified 76.4-77.8% with hosted Qwen 3.7 Max, and Qwen3.6's 73.4 came from Qwen's internal scaffold, not Qwen Code, so no local-model gain is measured [vendor] ([QwenLM/qwen-code](https://github.com/QwenLM/qwen-code); [evaluation](https://github.com/QwenLM/qwen-code/blob/main/docs/evaluation.md); [Qwen3.6 card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B)). In every option, "node only inside `docker compose exec app`" is a rule enforced by deny lists, not a harness feature [inference]. A harness that does not read `settings.json` therefore needs the guard rebuilt in its own syntax, or at the OS level. **Ruling (Yami, 2026-11-12): the pilot changes only the model.** The builder keeps Claude Code with the same rules and commands, so the results measure the model, one change at a time; another agent (Qwen Code first) is a later, separate change.

Routers are a later-iteration tool, and they collide with your no-upgrade constraint. **claude-code-router** (CCR, MIT, v3.1.x, about 37K stars) routes Claude Code request types (`default`, `background`, `think`, `longContext`) and specific subagents to different models. It also loses the prompt cache on a mid-session model switch, and other models produced malformed edit hunks ([morphllm](https://www.morphllm.com/claude-code-router)). **LiteLLM** shipped two malicious PyPI releases (1.82.7 and 1.82.8, 2026-03-24) that stole credentials, so it must be pinned and hash-checked ([developertoolkit.ai](https://developertoolkit.ai/en/ecosystem/agent-tools/gateways-local-models/)). More decisive: any routed setup sends the Claude side through an API credential billed per token, not through your Max login ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/llm-gateway)). The better fit for iteration 1 is **two separate processes with no router**: Claude Code on the subscription for planning and review, a separately launched local-endpoint Claude Code for the build, each in its own worktree, with the GitHub issue and PR as the hand-off. That is the builder-session/reviewer-session split you already run [inference; no source describes this exact setup]. The research did not cover Aider, Cline, Roo Code, Kilo Code, OpenHands, Continue, Qwen Code, Factory Droid or Amp. Treat them as unassessed, not rejected.

## A pre-registered forward queue beats replay and cherry-picking

The repo itself sets the bar. Gravity's review policy approves round 1 only when there are no findings at all, a Low included ([CLAUDE.md:113-116](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). Across 61 build PRs from #125 on, my notes' regex count gives a **median of 2 rounds, a mean of 2.61, round-1 APPROVED in only 6, and a third needing 3+ rounds**. PRs of 50 changed lines or fewer (n=23) averaged **2.0 rounds, maximum 3** [measured, approximate parse] ([PR reviews](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/pulls)). "Approved in round 1" is therefore the wrong gate, because Opus fails it about 90% of the time. The rules have also drifted fast and then stopped. `build-issue.md` grew about **10.8x, from 1,405 to 15,221 bytes, between 2026-10-16 and 2026-10-27**, and CLAUDE.md grew from 8,245 to 17,011 bytes. Nothing has touched CLAUDE.md or `.claude/commands/` since 2026-10-28, although `scripts/ci-local.sh` and the `/ui-walk` checks kept changing ([CLAUDE.md history](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/commits/main/CLAUDE.md); [build-issue.md history](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/commits/main/.claude/commands/build-issue.md)). Open issue **#505** proposes process changes you have already accepted, so the rules may move mid-pilot ([#505](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/issues/505)).

**Use the forward queue as the primary arm.** Write the eligibility rule before seeing any outcomes, then take the next N eligible issues in queue order. The rule: `Plan: none`, no UI change (no `/ui-walk`), no async-ordering logic, labelled low-priority or bug, and a "Done when" a check can run. Hand-picking "easy" issues after reading them reproduces the selection effect METR documents ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). A stated stratum is not cherry-picking, as long as the conclusion applies only to that stratum. The pool exists. Of 211 open issues, **62 carry `Plan: none`**, and about 14 are non-UI script or code bugs that fit a first pilot, such as #581 (restore.sh drops gzip's exit status), #598, #583, #584, #602, #576, #558 and #552 ([open issues](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/issues?q=is%3Aopen+label%3Alow-priority)). Several `Plan: none` bugs are async-ordering cases (#519, #521, #524), which the issue template itself rates as research-level, so exclude them ([issue template:58-62](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/blob/main/.github/ISSUE_TEMPLATE/issue.md)). Forward builds have one economic advantage: every Claude review they consume ships real work. Replays spend scarce review usage and deliver nothing.

**Use 3-5 replays only as paired calibration and a shakedown**, and judge them against today's rules. Judging under the rules of their time would predict nothing about building today, and restoring old scripts and reviewer behaviour costs more than it teaches [inference]. The fair protocol follows SWE-bench's unit, which is issue text plus the repo at the base commit, with no tests or fix visible ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)). Branch from the PR's **fork point** (the parent of its first commit). Do not use the merge parent, which already contains the D rows ruled during that build (D413), and do not trust GitHub's `base.sha`, which can point days later, as on #575. Overlay today's CLAUDE.md, commands, settings and scripts as one marked "harness" commit, but keep the old decision log so future rulings do not leak. Isolate each trial so it cannot `git log` its way to the answer, and Anthropic's agent-eval guidance warns about exactly that ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Run replays in a fork or scratch repo, not on `yamimaio/gravity`, so issue numbering and the duplicate-id CI check stay clean. As a control, re-review Opus's original PR head under today's `/review-pr` in the same week, and compare round-1 High and Medium counts per pair. That also measures reviewer-to-reviewer variance, which nobody knows yet. Good candidates are #464, #458, #395, #323 and #163. The fork-point rule matters because the private repo removes the usual threat: an open-weight model cannot have trained on these fixes, so the leakage risk lives in the repo's own later docs [inference].

**The gate needs hard stops, one binary primary outcome and secondary profiles.** **Hard stops**, any one of which pauses the pilot:

- a push to `main`, or a git change outside the builder's worktree;
- host `node`/`npm`/`npx`, or any `docker … prune`;
- a secret or user content in a log, commit or PR;
- a test that calls an external service;
- scope creep flagged by the reviewer.

The **primary outcome** is per issue: the owner merges it, with **review rounds ≤ the Opus baseline for its size bucket + 1** (≤3 for PRs of 50 lines or fewer), and **no Claude takeover**. Report it as a rate with an interval. **Highs are compared as a rate, not a per-issue fail** (Yami, 2026-11-12): the share of local PRs with a High in any round must be no worse than Opus's for the same size bucket, checked against the Opus control re-reviews. Opus had a High in 3 of 23 build PRs of 50 lines or fewer (#220, #416, #474) and 16 of 61 overall since #125 [measured, regex count over review text, approximate]. A per-issue "no High" bar would be one Opus itself misses, and fixing a High already costs extra rounds. As **secondaries**, tag every finding into one taxonomy (correctness, domain-spec or D-row conflict, test or evidence gap, process bookkeeping, scope, security; Yami chose these 6 over a 12-category list on 2026-11-12, so counts stay meaningful with about 10 builds) and compare the mix with the Opus controls. A model that matches Opus on correctness but loses on bookkeeping points to fixable harness or prompt work. The reverse points to a capability gap. Issue #505 already recorded a PR where **16 of 21 findings were bookkeeping**, so expect that category to dominate ([#505](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/issues/505)). Track Claude usage per merged issue, the owner's minutes and rulings per PR, and wall-clock time, but do not gate on time. CI and `scripts/ci-local.sh` are preconditions, not the gate. Label failures by hand from transcripts first, per Hamel Husain's "read traces" method, until new builds stop revealing new failure types ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-many-examples-do-i-need-for-an-eval.html)). Anthropic's advice to grade outcomes rather than paths applies too: do not score a build on how closely it matches Opus's diff ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).

**Ten forward builds can separate "mostly works" from "mostly fails". They cannot show that rare failures are rare.** By my calculation, 8 of 10 successes gives a 95% Wilson interval of about **49-94%**. Zero hard-stop violations in 10 builds only bounds the true rate below about **26%** (exact binomial; the rule of three gives 30%) ([rule of three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_%28statistics%29)). Hard stops must therefore be prevented by guards, not by sample size. Five paired replays work only as a sanity check: even 5 of 5 in one direction is p≈0.03 one-sided [inference, per the paired-difference approach in [Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1)]. Anthropic suggests 20-50 tasks for an eval suite, and SWE-rebench runs each model 5 times on its set ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [SWE-rebench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20411v2)). A solo developer cannot afford either, so pre-register sequential stopping rules [inference]:

| Rule | When it applies | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Hard stop | Any build | Stop the pilot at once, or pause it until a fix is in. |
| Over the limit | Any build | A build that needs more rounds than the Opus baseline for its size bucket + 1 counts as a fail, but keeps going until it merges or reaches twice that limit (6 rounds for a PR of 50 lines or fewer). Past the cap, Claude takes over. The ledger records the extra rounds, the extra Claude usage and why each round failed (Yami, 2026-11-12). |
| Futility | After builds 4-5 | Pause and diagnose if 3 or more builds needed a Claude rescue, carried a High, or went over their size bucket's round limit: start no new builds, read the failure causes in the ledger, then either stop and record "not viable for this stratum" or name one change to test next as a new iteration (Yami, 2026-11-12). The limit is size-based because Opus itself needed 4+ rounds on 0 of 23 PRs of 50 lines or fewer, but on 4 of 16 at 51-400 lines and 5 of 22 above that [measured, approximate regex count]; "Plan: none" does not guarantee a small PR. |
| Success | After build 10 | Adopt for the stratum if 8 or more of 10 met the primary outcome, net Claude usage per merged issue clears the threshold set in Stage 0, and your minutes per PR did not rise. |
| Otherwise | After build 10 | Extend to 20 builds, or stop. |
| Saturation | At any point | Stop when no new failure type has appeared in 3-4 builds and the result is clearly on one side. |

Because local builds cost no Claude usage, building 2-3 issues twice gives a cheap consistency estimate (Anthropic's pass^k), and only the reviews of those second builds cost Claude usage. Plan and build run in separate sessions (Yami, 2026-11-12): a Claude session writes the plan and posts it on the issue, and the build runs in a fresh session that starts from the issue and that plan, for the local builder and for any Opus build it is compared with, so both builders start from the same context. For "Plan: none" issues the issue text is the plan. Freeze CLAUDE.md and the commands for the pilot's length (decided 2026-11-12: freeze once milestone 2's process changes land), or record the build index where they changed and analyse the before and after halves separately.

**The reviewer is blind to who built the PR, and the builder is disclosed after the verdict** (Yami, 2026-11-12). This applies to both arms, Opus controls included. The builder session leaves the attribution lines out of the PR body and the commit trailers. The run ledger records the builder from the start. Once the reviewer posts its verdict, a PR comment adds "Built by: <model>". LLM judges change their verdicts based on source labels, usually harsher on work labelled as AI-made: 13 LLMs penalised AI-labelled creative writing about 2.5 times more than humans did ([arXiv 2510.08831](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08831)). GPT-4o and Gemini rank work Expert > Human > LLM > unlabelled, and their written reasons never cite the label ([arXiv 2509.26072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26072)). Every model tested, Claude Opus 4.5 included, rated answers labelled "human-written" higher ([arXiv 2604.05593](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.05593)). Blinding does not remove self-preference: LLM judges favour unlabelled output that reads like their own ([arXiv 2404.13076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). That effect would show up as fewer findings per line on the Opus controls, which the ledger can measure. No study tests either effect on code review by an LLM. A study of 447 human engineers found that disclosing AI use did not change their code ratings (Butler, Murphy-Hill et al., VL/HCC 2026).

## The build-to-review usage ratio decides whether any of this pays

**First measurement (2026-11-13).** These figures come from Yami's Claude Code logs for 2026-10-03 to 2026-11-12, read with `research/pilot/usage-baseline/session_usage.py`. There were 333 Gravity sessions: 78 builds, 231 reviews and 24 other. 69 issues have both a build and a review. Per issue, the median build costs about **4 review rounds** (middle half 2.9-6.7). Builder-side usage is **about two-thirds** of build plus review usage, both in October and in November. A perfect local builder would therefore save up to about 66%. At the 20% floor, local builds could add up to about 1.4 times today's review usage (about 4.5 extra rounds per issue) and still pass. The kill check (*B*/*R* below about 0.5) passes by a wide margin. Limits:
- Until 2026-11-12 this *B* includes planning, which stays with Claude under the plan/build split. If planning were a third of *B*, the ceiling would be about 44%.
- Usage is counted in price-weighted tokens, not split by model; 323 of the 333 sessions used Opus.
- 12 sessions were tagged from free-text prompts, so their tags are low-confidence.
- All of this ran under the old rules. The baseline that counts is milestone 2 under the frozen rules.

The data is in `sessions-tagged.csv` and `issues-br.csv` in the same folder.

No source has measured subscription savings from offloading builds to a local model. The best quantitative routing case fell from **about $100 to $15-20 per run**, but it used cheap hosted models and did not measure quality [anecdote] ([Arize](https://arize.com/blog/how-i-cut-coding-agent-costs-with-model-harness-routing/)). The same source warns that "a cheaper model can produce a more expensive run if it uses more tokens, retries often, or needs heavy repair." The arithmetic for Gravity shows why [inference]. Let *B* be all the builder-side Claude usage for an issue, meaning the first build plus every fix push between review rounds and any decision PR the builder drafts (clarified 2026-11-12 after Yami asked whether fixes count; with a local builder all of it moves to the Mac, and planning is a Claude session in both setups, so it cancels out), and *R* one review round, including `/code-review high` and the subagents it may fork ([review-pr.md:14-20](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/blob/main/.claude/commands/review-pr.md)). A small PR's baseline is about *B* + 2.0*R*. A local build costs (2.0 + Δ)*R* plus any rescue, where Δ is the extra rounds it causes. The share of Claude usage saved is then (*B* − Δ*R*)/(*B* + 2*R*):

| *B*/*R* (unmeasured) | Saved if Δ = 0 | Δ = 0.5 | Δ = 1 | Largest Δ that still saves 20% (pass floor) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 33% | 17% | 0% | 0.4 |
| 2 | 50% | 38% | 25% | 1.2 |
| 4 | 67% | 58% | 50% | 2.8 |

These are illustrative ratios, not measurements. They show that **a success threshold written before measuring *B*/*R* is a guess**. If reviews already cost as much as builds, even a perfect local builder saves only a third. Pre-register the formula, and set the number after Stage 0. **Ruled (Yami, 2026-11-12):** the pass floor is 20% less Claude usage per issue, which frees one day in five for another project; the final value is set at the end of Stage 0, once *B*/*R* is measured and before any build runs. The longer-term goal is 3x the work, which later iterations can work towards.

Measure *B* and *R* in tokens and API-equivalent dollars, keeping in mind that **on Max these dollar figures are estimates, not bills**. `/usage` shows plan bars and a 24-hour or 7-day breakdown by skill, subagent and MCP server, built from local session history ([Claude Code docs, costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs.md)). **OpenTelemetry export** (`CLAUDE_CODE_ENABLE_TELEMETRY=1`) emits `claude_code.token.usage` by type and `claude_code.cost.usage` per `session.id`, and `OTEL_RESOURCE_ATTRIBUTES` can tag every metric with role, issue and round (for example `pilot=local1,role=review,issue=581,round=1`) ([Claude Code docs, monitoring](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/monitoring-usage)). **ccusage** reads `~/.claude/projects` JSONL logs and reports per session with `--json`, but has no project filter ([ccusage session docs](https://www.mintlify.com/ryoppippi/ccusage/api/commands/session)). Builder and reviewer sessions both start in the main checkout, so you must map sessions to roles by id, date and first prompt [inference]. Two traps follow. First, **session data older than 30 days is deleted by default** (`cleanupPeriodDays`), so the history back to about 2026-10-12 disappears day by day, and recent Opus builds such as #575, #608, #537 and #541 should be exported now ([Claude Code docs, costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs.md)). Second, `npx ccusage` on the host is the kind of call RULES 10 forbids in a repo session ([CLAUDE.md:87](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/blob/main/CLAUDE.md)). Run it outside any Gravity session with your explicit OK, or in a throwaway container with `~/.claude` mounted read-only, or rely on OTEL alone [inference]. The local builder's own sessions also land in `~/.claude/projects`, and ccusage may price them at Claude rates, so filter them out by model name.

Count every Claude cost a local build causes: review rounds and their forks, any rescue or fix-up session, decision PRs Claude drafts, and your own Claude-assisted triage. Capacity has two more non-Claude costs [inference]. The laptop is occupied: the model, the builder's stack and any reviewer stack share 64 GB, and the Docker Desktop VM's allocation was not researched. Your attention is spent too: rulings asked, comments written and fixes after an AI `APPROVED`, as on #323 ([PR #323](https://github.com/yamimaio/gravity/pull/323)). A drop in Claude usage that raises your minutes per PR is not a win.

The setup, method and failure lessons carry over; the pass rate, the saving and the safety cases do not. **Local planning has little payoff**, because planning uses few tokens and carries a lot of weight. **Local review should come last or never**, because the review is the quality gate and the evidence for small models on judgment tasks is the weakest.

## Conclusion

The question has shifted from "is a local model good enough to build?" to "is the build a big enough share of Claude usage to be worth taking away?" On capability, the answer for small, well-specified, non-UI issues is now plausibly yes. A 3B-active MoE model in your own harness sits near late-2025 Claude on the one comparable board, and quantization no longer costs much. On economics, the answer is unknown, and Gravity's strict review loop is the reason. It is a quality gate few organisations have, and it charges every weaker build in frontier review rounds. The pilot's most valuable output is therefore the *B*/*R* ratio and a measured round inflation, Δ. Together they say whether any builder swap, local or hosted, can pay off in a review-heavy process.

That same finding is the leadership story worth telling. It is not "local models are ready". It is a measured pilot that put the stopping rule before the results, counted review effort and the owner's minutes as costs, checked data locality instead of trusting it, and found that the binding constraint was the review process, not the model. That pattern carries to any team. The win from AI builders is capped by what it costs to verify their work, so the next lever after the builder is cheaper verification: escalation checkpoints, mechanical pre-review, and a trimmed rulebook a smaller model can follow.
